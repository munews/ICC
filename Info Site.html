☪ ROOT: ISLAM
Branch 1: Sunni Islam
Largest branch (~85% of Muslims). Community elects leaders. Four major jurisprudential schools: Hanafi, Hanbali, Maliki, Shafi'i.
↓ subdivisions ↓
Salafism
Ultra-conservative fundamentalism. Literalist interpretation of Qur'an & Sunnah. Claims "pure" original Islam. Used by: AQIM, AD
Wahhabism
Literal Qur'an interpretation. Rejects "innovations" and polytheism. Absolute monotheism emphasis.
Sufism
Mystical inner dimension of Islam. Spiritual experience focus. Exists in both Sunni & Shia traditions. Targeted by jihadists.

Branch 2: Shia Islam
~15% of Muslims. Leadership remains within the Prophet's family. Different legal schools and theology.
↓
Shia Schools
Twelver, Ismaili, Zaidi — each with distinct legal & theological traditions.

⚖ Law: Sharia

Islamic moral code and legal framework governing daily rituals, personal ethics, family law, finance, and criminal justice. Imposed by AD and AQIM in Timbuktu during the Al Hassan period.

Alliance MNLA + AD + AQIM
Jan – Mar 2012
Common enemy: the Malian government. MNLA seeks Tuareg independence; jihadists seek a religious state. Despite opposite ideologies, they coordinate the northern takeover. A marriage of convenience.
Breakdown MNLA vs AD + AQIM
April 2012
MNLA is secular nationalist; jihadists want Islamic governance. AD and AQIM expel MNLA from Timbuktu on 2 April — one day after MNLA captured it. This is the critical turning point.
Tense Coexistence MNLA + AQIM + AD
Apr – Jun 2012
Groups operate in the same regions independently. MNLA weakened and increasingly marginalized by jihadists. No unified command, ongoing friction.
Competition AQIM vs MUJAO vs AD
Jun 2012 – Jan 2013
MUJAO split from AQIM late 2011 over tactics and authority. Groups compete for territorial control and Islamic legitimacy. Periodic cooperation and conflict. Northern Mali splits into three jihadist zones.
Fragmented Control AQIM · AD · MUJAO · MNLA
Mid 2012 – Jan 2013
Timbuktu: AD + AQIM  |  Gao: MUJAO  |  Kidal: MNLA

Constant negotiation over territory. No unified jihadist command. MNLA effectively sidelined.
International All insurgents vs French/Malian Forces
Jan 2013 onward
French intervention (Operation Serval) creates a common external enemy. Jihadist groups loosely unite in opposition, but lack of unified command weakens their military response. Timbuktu liberated 28–29 January 2013.

Why the Alliance Failed — Core Contradiction

The Jan–Mar 2012 alliance was built on a single shared interest: defeating the Malian government. Once the north was taken, the alliance collapsed immediately because the groups' visions were irreconcilable:


MNLA wanted a secular Tuareg nation — no Sharia, no religious law, Tuareg identity-based governance.


AQIM + AD wanted an Islamic state governed by Sharia law, with no room for secular nationalism.


Within 24 hours of MNLA capturing Timbuktu (1 Apr), AD and AQIM expelled them (2 Apr). The city then entered the "Al Hassan Period" of strict Sharia enforcement.

The Al Hassan Case — Context Summary

Who: Al Hassan, military commander of AD forces in Timbuktu

Under: Iyad Ag Ghaly (AD founder/overall leader)

When: April 2012 – January 2013

Where: Timbuktu, northern Mali

What: AD and AQIM imposed Sharia law; established religious courts; committed acts prosecuted as crimes against humanity and war crimes

End: French liberation of Timbuktu, 28–29 January 2013

Arrest: 31 March 2018, Netherlands; prosecuted at ICC
